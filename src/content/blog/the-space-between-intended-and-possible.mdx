---
title: "The Space Between Intended and Possible"
description: "On systems, emergence, and the limits of intention"
date: 2026-10-01

We rarely understand the systems we operate within as well as we think we do. 

We understand pieces of them. We understand what they were designed to do, the parts we interact with, the rules we have written, the assumptions we have made, and the behaviors we have learned to expect. 
We build diagrams, abstractions, policies, and mental models because we need some way to make the whole thing understandable.

But those models are not the system.

A sufficiently complex system is simply too large to hold in our heads at once. Knowledge of it becomes fragmented. One person understands a service. Another understands an identity system. Someone else understands a workflow, a dependency, or a piece of infrastructure.
We understand the micro without the macro, or the macro without the details that produce it.

And where our understanding ends, assumptions begin.

We assume one component behaves the way we believe it does. We assume a relationship means what it was originally intended to mean. We assume a permission exists for the reason it was created. 
We assume the boundaries we have drawn around the system correspond to boundaries that actually exist.

We have to. Complexity is overwhelming, and abstraction is how we survive it.

In much the same way that our minds construct a workable picture of the world from limited sensory information, we construct workable models of the systems around us. 
We simplify. We infer. We ignore detail. We fill gaps. 

Most of the time, that works remarkably well. Until it doesn't.

Systems do not behave according to our understanding of them. They behave according to their structure. And structure contains possibility. The system we intended is relatively easy to describe. 
There are roles, boundaries, workflows, permissions, rules, and expected paths. Intention gives us something coherent to reason about. The possible system is harder.
It includes every behavior those pieces can produce together, every path created through their relationships, and every outcome that can emerge from their interaction.

Intended state is legible. Possible state is uncertain. And possibility is not static.

A new application is connected. A person changes roles. A service gains a permission. An automation begins acting on behalf of a user. One system begins trusting another. 
A decision made somewhere else quietly changes what is possible here. Often, nothing is broken. Every individual component may be behaving exactly as intended. The surprise exists in the composition.

Intent tends to be local. Possibility is compositional.

We create relationships one at a time, for reasons that make sense in isolation. The system combines them into paths that no one necessarily designed.
A decision in one part of the system changes the conditions somewhere else. Two systems can each behave correctly in isolation while producing something unexpected when they interact.
The behavior of the whole is not always obvious from the behavior of its parts.

And we are not standing outside of this system observing it. We are part of it.

Our decisions alter its structure. Our actions create new relationships. Our attempts to control the system change the environment that everyone else operates within.

This is where security becomes uncomfortable.

We are asked to maintain control over a system we do not fully know, against an adversary we often cannot see, while both the system and the people operating within it continue to change.
Security lives somewhere between the system we intended and the system that is actually possible.

Hackers offer an interesting way of looking at that space.

The word carries plenty of baggage, particularly in security, where it is often treated as synonymous with attacker. But there is something useful in the way hackers approach systems.
They cannot rely entirely on documentation, architecture diagrams, or the beliefs of the people who built the system. They do not trust the systems at face value. They are intrincisly curious.
They have to discover what it actually allows, the space of possibility. They attempt to alter it’s intended functionality to align with their goals.

A designer may ask:

**What is this system supposed to do?**

A hacker asks:

**What can I make this system do?**

In the security landscape, the answer is sometimes a software vulnerability. But it does not have to be.

It might be a sequence of perfectly legitimate permissions. A trust relationship. An overlooked workflow. Several harmless capabilities that become meaningful only when composed.

In that sense, hacking is less interesting as the act of breaking a system than as the exploration of possibility within it.

The hacker begins somewhere, wants to arrive somewhere else, and tries to understand what lies between the two:
**Where am I? What do I know? Where do I want to go? What does the system make possible between the two?**

Those questions are difficult precisely because the system is never completely known.
Perhaps that is the uncomfortable truth at the center of security.

We will never possess a perfect model of the systems we are trying to protect.
We will operate through incomplete information, imperfect abstractions, and assumptions that are sometimes wrong.
The challenge is not to eliminate that uncertainty.
It is to understand and act within it.
Because the system we intend is only one version of the system.

The rest exists in the space of possibility.
